Litrature Review Draft

Domain 1: Secure Internal Messaging & Insider Threats
Paper 1: "SoK: An Analysis of End-to-End Encryption and Authentication Ceremonies in Secure Messaging Systems" (2023)
Focus: Evaluating the security and usability of authentication ceremonies (like scanning QR codes) in 17 popular E2EE apps (WhatsApp, Signal, Zoom, etc.).
Problem Statement: While "Opportunistic" encryption (default) stops passive snooping, it fails against active Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) attacks. Existing manual "Authentication Ceremonies" are optional, hard to find, and prone to human error.
Method: Systematization of Knowledge (SoK) & Comparative Analysis. Analyzed 17 apps, reviewed white papers, and conducted usability tests on how users verify keys (fingerprints).
Findings: All current E2EE apps are vulnerable to active MitM attacks by default. None offer a "usable" mandatory authentication method, and users consistently fail to verify long numeric/hexadecimal strings correctly.
Strengths/Weaknesses:
Strength: Extremely comprehensive scope (17 apps) and clearly identifies "Human Error" as the weak link in current E2EE security.
Weakness: Diagnoses the problem effectively but suggests "recommendations" rather than building a novel technical solution.
Future Directions: Need for automated or semi-automated authentication ceremonies to remove the burden from the user.
Table Data: Domain: Secure Messaging | Method: SoK/Analysis | Outcome: Current auth ceremonies are broken/unused.
Synthesis Tip: "As demonstrated by Alatawi and Saxena (2023), reliance on manual 'Authentication Ceremonies' leaves major platforms vulnerable to active MitM attacks. SentriZK addresses this critical gap by automating trust verification via Zero-Knowledge Proofs, eliminating the risk of human error."
Paper 2: "Insider Threat Detection and Secure Data Transfer Leveraging Bidirectional LSTM" (2025)
1. Focus: Using advanced Deep Learning (Bi-LSTM) to detect malicious insiders who are already inside the network.
2. Problem Statement: Traditional security focuses on firewalls (external threats), but "insiders" (employees) often bypass these checks. Standard detection methods struggle to catch subtle behavioral changes in encrypted traffic.
3. Method: Proposed a Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM) network. This is a type of Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) that learns patterns in data sequences (forward and backward) to predict if a user's behavior is normal or malicious.
4. Findings: The Bi-LSTM model achieved higher accuracy than standard machine learning models because it understands the "context" of user actions over time, not just single events.
5. Strengths/Weaknesses:
Strength: High detection accuracy for time-series data (user behavior logs).
Weakness: Deep learning models like LSTM are computationally heavy and usually require a server (not ideal for mobile/edge).
6. Future Directions: Improving the speed of training and testing on diverse datasets.
7. Table Data: Domain: Insider Threat | Method: Bi-LSTM | Outcome: Improved Accuracy.
8. Synthesis Tip: Link this to SentriZK: "While [Paper 1] proves LSTM is effective for threat detection, SentriZK adapts this concept to mobile devices by using a lighter model (TFLite) to preserve battery life."
Paper 3: "A Review of Recent Advances, Challenges, and Opportunities in Malicious Insider Threat Detection Using Machine Learning Methods" (2024)
1. Focus: A comprehensive survey (review) of all current ML techniques used to catch insider threats.
2. Problem Statement: There is no single "perfect" algorithm; different attacks require different detection methods. Also, balancing False Positives (flagging innocent users) vs. False Negatives (missing attackers) is a major challenge.
3. Method: Systematic Literature Review (SLR). The authors analyzed dozens of papers to categorize techniques into Supervised, Unsupervised, and Deep Learning.
4. Findings: Deep Learning is becoming the standard, but it lacks "explainability" (we don't know why the AI flagged a user). Data imbalance (too few examples of attacks) makes training hard.
5. Strengths/Weaknesses:
Strength: Excellent overview of the "state of the art."
Weakness: Does not propose a new tool, only reviews existing ones.
6. Future Directions: Need for "Explainable AI" (XAI) and privacy-preserving detection methods.
7. Table Data: Domain: Survey | Method: SLR | Outcome: Taxonomy of ML methods.
8. Synthesis Tip: Use this to justify your specific choice of ML algorithm. "As highlighted by [Paper 2], data imbalance is a challenge; SentriZK addresses this by using pre-trained models..."
Paper 4: "Threat Modeling for Enterprise Secure Messaging Applications" (2025)
1. Focus: Identifying specific security holes in apps like Slack, Teams, and WhatsApp Enterprise.
2. Problem Statement: Enterprise apps are complex. They mix file sharing, video, and chat. This complexity creates new attack surfaces (e.g., malicious file uploads, link previews) that simple "encryption" cannot fix.
3. Method: Threat Modeling (STRIDE or similar). The authors systematically mapped out architecture diagrams of chat apps and identified where attacks could happen.
4. Findings: End-to-End Encryption (E2EE) protects data in transit, but metadata (who spoke to whom) and endpoint security (the phone itself) remain vulnerable.
5. Strengths/Weaknesses:
Strength: Very specific to your exact domain (chat apps).
Weakness: Focuses on finding problems rather than building a solution.
6. Future Directions: Recommends better endpoint verification and minimizing metadata collection.
7. Table Data: Domain: Messaging Security | Method: Threat Modeling | Outcome: Identified endpoint vulnerabilities.
8. Synthesis Tip: This is your "Why". "Following the threat model analysis by [Paper 3], SentriZK focuses on securing the endpoint (the mobile app) since the network layer is already encrypted."

Domain 2: Zero-Knowledge Proofs (Authentication)
Paper 1: "Enhancing Digital Privacy: The Application of Zero-Knowledge Proofs in Authentication Systems" (2024)
1. Focus: The application of Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs), specifically zk-SNARKs and zk-STARKs, as a solution to replace traditional password-based authentication.
2. Problem Statement: Traditional authentication methods (like password hashing) expose credentials to theft and rely on centralized databases that create single points of failure. There is a conflict between the need for security and the need to preserve user privacy.
3. Method: Comparative Analysis Methodology. The authors contrasted ZKP-based systems against traditional authentication using specific metrics: computational efficiency, scalability, and degree of privacy preservation.
4. Findings: ZKP systems offer superior privacy because they verify users without transmitting any private data. The paper highlights that zk-STARKs improve upon zk-SNARKs by removing the need for a "trusted setup" and offering quantum resistance, though they currently have larger proof sizes.
5. Strengths/Weaknesses:
Strength: Clearly distinguishes between zk-SNARKs (efficient, small proofs) and zk-STARKs (transparent, no trusted setup), helping justify protocol choices.
Weakness: Acknowledges that high computational complexity and the requirement for specialized cryptographic knowledge are major barriers to widespread adoption.
6. Future Directions: Research should focus on developing user-friendly frameworks and APIs to lower the entry barrier for developers, and optimizing protocols to run efficiently on standard hardware.
7. Table Data: Domain: ZKP Authentication | Method: Comparative Analysis | Outcome: ZKP offers superior privacy but requires UI/UX improvements.
8. Synthesis Tip: "Bhattacharya et al. (2024) emphasize that while ZKPs provide superior privacy by eliminating password transmission, widespread adoption is hindered by complexity. SentriZK addresses this by wrapping the complex zk-SNARK protocol inside a user-friendly mobile interface, as recommended in their future directions."
Paper 2: "A Survey on Zero-Knowledge Authentication for Internet of Things" (2023)
1. Focus: How to use ZKP on weak devices like IoT sensors (which are similar to mobile phones in terms of battery/CPU limits).
2. Problem Statement: IoT devices have low battery and memory. Standard ZKP (like older protocols) is too heavy/slow for them.
3. Method: Survey & Comparative Analysis. Compared different ZKP schemes (zk-SNARKs vs. Sigma protocols) based on computation cost and energy usage.
4. Findings: Non-interactive Zero-Knowledge Proofs (NI-ZKP) are best for devices with unstable internet. zk-SNARKs (Groth16) are identified as highly efficient due to small proof size.
5. Strengths/Weaknesses:
Strength: Directly validates the use of lightweight ZKP on restricted hardware.
Weakness: Focuses on IoT sensors, not specifically smartphones (though the math is the same).
6. Future Directions: Optimizing the "Setup Phase" of zk-SNARKs to be less dependent on a trusted third party.
7. Table Data: Domain: ZKP/IoT | Method: Survey | Outcome: NI-ZKP is best for low-power devices.
8. Synthesis Tip: "Since mobile devices share resource constraints with IoT, SentriZK adopts the efficiency recommendations from [Paper 4], utilizing Groth16 for minimal battery impact."
Paper 3: "Privacy-Preserving Identity Management System on Blockchain Using zk-SNARK" (2023)
1. Focus: Proving identity without revealing it, using Blockchain and zk-SNARKs.
2. Problem Statement: Centralized Identity Providers (like "Log in with Google") track user activity. Users lose privacy.
3. Method: System Implementation. Built a prototype using Ethereum (Blockchain) and ZoKrates/Circom (ZKP toolkit) to create a "Self-Sovereign Identity" system.
4. Findings: The system successfully verified users without storing passwords or personal data. Gas costs (transaction fees) were the main bottleneck, but the privacy was absolute.
5. Strengths/Weaknesses:
Strength: Practical implementation details (code, tools used) that you can reference.
Weakness: Blockchain focus might be "overkill" for a simple chat app, but the ZKP part is relevant.
6. Future Directions: reducing the computational cost of generating proofs on the client side.
7. Table Data: Domain: Identity/ZKP | Method: Prototype | Outcome: Complete privacy preservation.
8. Synthesis Tip: "SentriZK leverages the identity hiding properties demonstrated in [Paper 5], ensuring that even the chat server cannot link a session to a real-world identity."
Paper 4: "A Secure Framework for Privacy-Preserving Analytics in Healthcare Records Using Zero-Knowledge Proofs" (2025)
1. Focus: Applying ZKP to highly sensitive data (Medical Records) to prove data validity without leaking patient info.
2. Problem Statement: Healthcare data needs to be analyzed for research, but privacy laws (HIPAA/GDPR) make sharing hard.
3. Method: Framework Design. Proposed a system where hospitals generate ZKPs that prove "This patient has disease X" without revealing the patient's name or ID.
4. Findings: ZKP allows for "Auditability without Visibility"—you can check the math is correct without seeing the data.
5. Strengths/Weaknesses:
Strength: Demonstrates high-stakes trust in ZKP (if it's good enough for hospitals, it's good for chat).
Weakness: Complex setup required for multiple organizations.
6. Future Directions: Integrating this with Federated Learning.
7. Table Data: Domain: Privacy/Healthcare | Method: Framework | Outcome: Auditability without data leakage.
8. Synthesis Tip: "Similar to the healthcare framework in [Paper 6], SentriZK ensures 'Auditability without Visibility,' proving a user is authorized without revealing their password."

Domain 3: AI Anomaly Detection (On-Device/Lightweight)
Paper 1: "A Systematic Review of Anomaly detection using Machine and Deep Learning Techniques" (2022)
1. Focus: A comprehensive review of Machine Learning (SVM, k-NN) and Deep Learning (CNN, LSTM, Autoencoders) techniques used to detect anomalies across various domains (IoT, Video, Network traffic).
2. Problem Statement: Detecting anomalous activities is challenging due to the "unknown nature of anomalies" (they don't look like known attacks), noise in real-world data, and the inability of static algorithms to adapt to changing environmental conditions.
3. Method: Systematic Literature Review (SLR). The authors analyzed research papers published between 2019–2021, categorizing them by algorithm type (ML vs. DL) and application domain to evaluate their strengths and weaknesses.
4. Findings: Deep Learning (DL) models generally outperform traditional Machine Learning in handling high-dimensional data (like images or complex logs). However, DL models like LSTM require significant bandwidth and time resources, whereas models like SVM often underfit when data is complex.
5. Strengths/Weaknesses:
Strength: Provides a specific critique of One-Class SVM (it struggles with noisy datasets), which justifies your choice to use more advanced Deep Learning methods.
Weakness: Notes that high accuracy in Deep Learning often comes at the cost of high computational power, posing a challenge for real-time detection.
6. Future Directions: The authors suggest improving performance in limited resource environments (like IoT/Mobile) by utilizing low-cost sensors and optimizing algorithms to work with smaller sampled data.
7. Table Data: Domain: Anomaly Detection | Method: Systematic Review | Outcome: Deep Learning handles complex data better than traditional ML.
8. Synthesis Tip: "Natha et al. (2022) highlight that traditional ML methods like SVM often underfit complex, noisy data. Consequently, SentriZK leverages Deep Learning (TensorFlow Lite) to capture high-dimensional behavioral patterns, addressing the resource constraints of mobile devices as suggested in their review of future directions."
Paper 2: "AI-Driven Smishing Detection in Android Devices Using TinyBERT and Aquila Optimization" (2025)
Focus: Detecting SMS phishing (Smishing) specifically on Android devices using lightweight Deep Learning models.
Problem Statement: Traditional rule-based spam filters miss context-heavy phishing attacks. Standard Deep Learning models (like full BERT) are too computationally heavy for mobile phones.
Method: Hybrid Deep Learning. Utilized TinyBERT for contextual feature extraction and Aquila Optimization (AO) to automatically tune hyperparameters for maximum efficiency on mobile.
Findings: The model achieved 96.81% accuracy with high precision (96.76%), significantly outperforming traditional models like SVM, LSTM, and GRU. It proved effective at reducing false positives.
Strengths/Weaknesses:
Strength: High accuracy specifically tailored for the Android platform, validating your choice of OS.
Weakness: While "Tiny," BERT-based models can still be heavier than non-transformer models (like Random Forest) if battery optimization isn't perfect.
Future Directions: Integrating this detection engine into real-time prevention systems to block attacks instantly.
Table Data: Domain: Mobile AI | Method: TinyBERT + Optimization | Outcome: 96.81% Accuracy on Android.
Synthesis Tip: "Validating the feasibility of on-device AI, Gaurav et al. (2025) achieved 96% accuracy in Android smishing detection using optimized models. SentriZK adopts this architecture, deploying lightweight inference models locally to ensure real-time protection without compromising user privacy."

1. Focus: Running phishing detection directly on mobile phones (Edge Computing).
2. Problem Statement: Most anti-phishing tools run in the Cloud. This introduces Latency (slow check times) and Privacy Risks (sending your URLs to a server).
3. Method: Experimental Implementation. Developed a compressed Deep Learning model optimized for mobile processors (ARM architecture).
4. Findings: Achieved high accuracy (90%+) with very low latency (<100ms) and minimal battery drain. Proved that "heavy" cloud servers aren't always needed.
5. Strengths/Weaknesses:
Strength: This is the exact validation for your project's AI module.
Weakness: Limited to URL detection (doesn't check file attachments).
6. Future Directions: Expanding the model to detect SMS phishing (Smishing) and QR codes.
7. Table Data: Domain: Mobile AI | Method: Experiment | Outcome: Low latency, high privacy.
8. Synthesis Tip: "SentriZK mirrors the architecture of PHILDER [Paper 7], deploying a TensorFlow Lite model locally to eliminate the latency and privacy risks of cloud-based scanning."

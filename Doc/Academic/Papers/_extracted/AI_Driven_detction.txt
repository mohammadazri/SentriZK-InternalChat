AI-Driven Smishing Detection in Android Devices
Using TinyBERT and Aquila Optimization
Akshat Gaurav1, Brij B. Gupta2∗, Kwok Tai Chui3
1Ronin Institute, Montclair, NJ, USA
2Department of Computer Science and Information Engineering, Asia University, Taichung 413, Taiwan

akshat.gaurav@ieee.org, bbgupta@asia.edu.tw, jktchui@hkmu.edu.hk
Abstract—The increasing prevalence of smishing (SMS phish-
ing) attacks on mobile devices poses significant security risks,
particularly for Android users. Traditional spam detection tech-
niques often fail to accurately distinguish between legitimate and
malicious messages due to the complexity and contextual nature
of smishing. In this paper, we propose an AI-driven smishing
detection approach using TinyBERT for feature extraction and
Aquila Optimization (AO) to optimize a deep learning model.
With AO-tuned hyperparameters, the proposed model achieved
an accuracy of 96.81%, outperforming standard models like
GRU, LSTM, and SVM. Our approach offers a robust, efficient
solution for Android smishing detection.
Keywords—Smishing Detection, TinyBERT, Aquila Optimiza-
tion, Android Security, SMS Phishing
I. INTRODUCTION
The proliferation of smartphones has significantly trans-
formed communication and information access, leading to
an increased vulnerability to various cybersecurity threats,
particularly smishing (SMS phishing) attacks. As smartphones
have become ubiquitous, with billions of users globally, they
have also become prime targets for cybercriminals. The rise
in mobile device usage has expanded the attack surface for
malicious activities, including phishing, malware, and unau-
thorized access to sensitive information[1, 2, 3]
Smishing attacks exploit the convenience of SMS, a com-
munication method that is widely used for personal, financial,
and business purposes. Research indicates that these attacks
have surged, with reports showing that a substantial percentage
of organizations have encountered smishing attempts. For
instance, a report by Proofpoint highlighted that 81% of
surveyed organizations in the U.S. faced such attacks in 2020,
underscoring the severity of the threat [4, 5, 6]. The nature of
smishing attacks often involves sending deceptive messages
that prompt users to click on malicious links or provide
sensitive information, making it a particularly insidious form
of phishing [7, 8].
A. Contribution
The contribution of this paper is a novel, AI-driven approach
for smishing detection in Android devices, utilizing TinyBERT
for effective feature extraction and Aquila Optimization (AO)
for hyperparameter tuning. This method enhances detection ac-
curacy while reducing false positives by leveraging optimized

II. RELATED WORK
Akande et al. [9] proposed a rule-based mobile application
for detecting and preventing smishing attacks by intercepting
incoming SMS and forwarding them through an API to a
rule-based machine learning model for classification. Gupta
et al. [10] proposes a hybrid deep learning model leveraging
feature selection and Ant Lion Optimization for hyperparam-
eter tuning, achieving a detection accuracy of 97% for IoT
cyberattacks. Zhou et al. [11] introduces a deep learning-based
phishing detection model for Semantic Web systems, leverag-
ing MobileBERT for feature extraction and covariance matrix
adaptation evolution strategy (CMA-ES) for hyperparameter
optimization, achieving a classification accuracy of 95%.
Joo et al. [12] proposed an enhanced security model called
“S-Detector” that utilizes a Naive Bayes classifier for de-
tecting smishing attacks in mobile computing environments.
By filtering SMS messages through statistical learning, the
model distinguishes between normal and smishing messages,
effectively addressing security concerns in mobile environ-
ments. Gupta et al. [13] proposes a novel phishing detection
model that integrates Random Forest for feature selection
and the Brown-Bear Optimization Algorithm (BBOA) for
hyperparameter tuning of a CNN, achieving 93% accuracy
and 92% precision. Gaurav et al. [14] proposes an AI-
driven Semantic Web phishing detection framework for e-
commerce systems, integrating Chi-square for feature selection
and the JADE algorithm for hyperparameter optimization of a
CNN, achieving 93% accuracy with consistent stability across
epochs.
Mehmood et al. [15] proposed a deep learning approach
combining Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) and Long

an exceptionally high accuracy of 99.97%, and identifies key

III. PROPOSED WORK
Our proposed smishing detection method (Figure 1) first
gathered labeled valid and spam texts from a publicly available
Kaggle SMS dataset. We cleaned, standardized the data by
removing special characters, digits, and punctuation, trans-
lating text to lowercase, eliminating stopwords, and apply-
ing lemmatization in the step of data preprocessing. Using
a lightweight transformer model, TinyBERT, we extracted
contextual embeddings of SMS messages. T-SNE visualization
of these embeddings confirmed good feature separation. By
balancing exploration and exploitation in the hyperparameter
search space, the Aquila Optimization (AO) method helped us
to finally maximize the hyperparameters of our deep learning
model, hence producing excellent smishing detection results.

tured pipeline from data preprocessing to hyperparameter

each stage.

IV. RESULT AND DISCUSSION
A. Dataset Representation
Widely utilized for SMS spam detection research, the
SMS Spam Collection dataset used for this study came
from Kaggle[21]. There are 5,574 English SMS messages
altogether, tagged as either ”ham,” (legal) or ”spam.” Two
columns—”v1” for the label and ”v2” for the SMS con-
tents—represent every communication. The dataset combines
messages coming from many sources: 425 spam messages
extracted from the Grumbletext website, a UK-based forum
where users report SMS spam; 3,450 legitimate messages from
the NUS SMS Corpus, mostly contributed by students at the
National University of Singapore; 450 legitimate messages
sourced from Caroline Tag’s PhD thesis; and 1,424 messages
(1,002 legitimate and 322 spam) from the SMS Spam Corpus
v.0.1 Big, previously used in academic research. Training and
testing smishing detection algorithms can be based on this
extensive collection of SMS data with dependability.
B. Dataset Preprocessing
We carried out many steps to standardize and clean the
SMS text data in the stage of dataset preparation. To rid any
non-alphabetic symbols that would not add to the meaning of
the communications, we first deleted special characters, digits,
and punctuation. We then turned all of the text downcase
to preserve consistency and prevent word duplication across
several contexts. Common stopwords (like ”the,” ”is,” and
”and”) that do not significantly provide semantic meaning for
our study were then deleted. At last, we lemmatized—that is,
stemmed—reducing words to their root forms, so aggregating
related terms (e.g., ”running” and ”runs” become ”run”). This
increases the meaning of the data.
Following preprocessing, we created a word cloud (Figure
2) to show the most often occurring terms in the SMS corpus.
Prominent in both valid (ham) and spam messages, the word
cloud exposes often used words such ”call,” ”get,” ”go,”
”free,” and ”need.” This graphic shows phrases that might
be especially important for differentiating smishing (spam)
from valid communications and offers understanding of the
language patterns found in SMS data.

BERT in the feature extraction phase. Essential for differen-
tiating between smishing (spam) and legal (ham) messages,

D. Performance of Aquila Optimization (AO)
Following TinyBERT feature extraction, we constructed a
deep learning model tuned with Aquila Optimization (AO)
method to identify the best hyperparameters. The next charts
show AO’s performance all through the optimization process.
• The runtime chart (Figure 4a) displaying in seconds the
time taken for every iteration of AO. The differences
in runtime show the computing load AO needs to in-
vestigate and use various hyperparameter settings. The
runtime stays rather constant, with small variations as the
method tries to optimally balance stages of exploration
and exploitation.
• Diversity Measurement Chart (Figure 4b) Tracking the
variety of solutions over iterations, the diversity mea-
surement chart offers understanding of the search space
exploration. The variety is first more reflective of the
exploratory phase of the algorithm. Diversity reduces as
AO advances, suggesting a concentration on exploitation;
AO then narrows down on likely areas of the solution
space to hone the ideal parameters.
• Percentage of Exploration vs. Exploitation (Figure 4c)
chart shows how to strike the balance between exploita-
tion and discovery. AO gives exploration top priority
early in the optimization process in order to cover a
large spectrum of feasible solutions. As the algorithm
converges, the focus moves to exploitation, guaranteeing
that the best solutions are polished further for highest
accuracy in hyperparameter choice.
• Global Objectives Chart (Figure 4d) Across iterations,
this chart shows the objective function values. The de-
clining trend shows AO’s achievement in reducing the
objective function, thereby obtaining ever better hyper-
parameter values. The stabilization at the end indicates
that AO has most likely attained ideal or almost ideal
conditions for the deep learning model.
E. Performance of Deep Learning Model
Following Aquila Optimization (AO) to find the best hyper-
parameters, we trained the deep learning model for five epochs
to preserve a lightweight and effective structure fit for Android
devices. A confusion matrix and a classification report helped
one assess the performance of the model.
Figure 5 presents the confusion matrix. The model appropri-
ately found 119 spam messages and 882 valid (ham) messages
in this matrix. Still, there were 26 false negatives—spam mails
misclassified as legitimate—and 7 false positives—legitimate
messages misclassified as spam. With appropriate performance
for spam detection as well, the great number of accurate
classifications—especially in the genuine class—indicate the
model’s robustness in spotting ham messages.
Classification Report For every class is presented in Figure
6, the classification report offers comprehensive measures
including F1-score, recall, and precision. The model minimizes
false positives in both classifications rather well: 97% for
authentic messages and 94% for spam. At 82%, the recall for
spam messages was rather lower, suggesting some space for
development in terms of all spam occurrence capture. With
an F1-score of 0.98 for valid communications and 0.88 for
spam, the model’s overall accuracy—which shows a strong
mix between precision and recall—was 97%.

V. CONCLUSION
In this study, we proposed an AI-driven smishing detection
model for Android devices, leveraging TinyBERT for feature
extraction and Aquila Optimization (AO) for hyperparameter
tuning. The model was trained on a publicly available SMS
dataset from Kaggle, with rigorous preprocessing to improve
message clarity. Visualization of TinyBERT embeddings using
t-SNE demonstrated clear feature separability between legit-
imate and spam messages. The optimized model achieved
superior performance, with 96.81% accuracy, 96.76% preci-
sion, 96.81% recall, and a 96.71% F1 score, outperforming
standard models like GRU, LSTM, and SVM. These results
underscore the model’s robustness and efficiency in detecting
smishing, providing a highly accurate solution for enhancing
mobile security against SMS phishing attacks in Android
environments.

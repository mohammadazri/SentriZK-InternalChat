ABSTRACT
The increasing sophistication of phishing attacks represents a significant challenge to
cybersecurity, particularly in the context of fraudulent emails crafted to deceive users and steal confidential
information. This paper proposes a deep learning-based approach for the automatic detection of phishing
in emails, the PHILDER (PHishing Intelligent Lightweight DEtection on Resource-limited devices) model.
The study employs a dataset composed of real emails extracted from PhishTank and the SpamAssassin
Public Corpus, spanning both legitimate and fraudulent samples. To address the natural imbalance between
spam and phishing emails, two strategies were applied: undersampling, which reduces the number of
legitimate emails to balance the classes, and oversampling using SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-
sampling Technique) to generate new synthetic samples of the minority class. Five models (ALBERT,
DistilBERT, MobileBERT, MiniLM, and TinyBERT) were trained: one using the original dataset, one
with undersampling, and a third with oversampling. The experimental results show that the oversampling
approach had to be discarded due to computational cost issues, the tendency to overfit, and inferior
performance compared to the other two approaches. The main contributions of this paper include the
application of computational efficiency metrics for model evaluation, which not only consider traditional
metrics but also enable assessment in real-world applications under hardware computational constraints,
and the exploration of different data composition strategies for training. The use of PHILDER (TinyBERT
combined with data balancing techniques) demonstrates a promising approach for the automatic detection
of phishing in emails, contributing to strengthening digital security against social engineering attacks.
INDEX TERMS Phishing, fraud detection, deep learning, BERT, cybersecurity.

However, the practical deployment of these models often
faces significant challenges, particularly in environments
with limited computational resources, such as IoT devices,
mobile phones, or embedded systems. Traditional deep
learning models, especially those based on large Transformer
architectures, typically demand substantial processing power
(CPUs, GPUs, TPUs) and memory, making their direct
implementation on resource-constrained devices impractical
or inefficient. This limitation arises from several factors:
• High Computational Cost: Complex neural networks
involve millions or even billions of parameters, requiring
extensive matrix multiplications and convolutions dur-
ing inference. This translates to high CPU/GPU cycles
and energy consumption, which are scarce resources in
edge devices.
• Large Memory Footprint: Storing model parameters and
intermediate activations consumes significant memory.
Resource-constrained devices often have limited RAM
and storage, making it impossible to load and run large
models.
• Latency Requirements: Many real-time cybersecurity
applications, such as phishing detection, require low-
latency responses. Large models can introduce consid-
erable inference delays, rendering them unsuitable for
scenarios where immediate action is critical.

Studies in the field demonstrate that highly effective
phishing detection can be achieved using only the textual
body of emails as the data source. Approaches combining
Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques such as
lemmatization and Bag-of-Words (BoW) vectorization with
topic models like Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) have
shown excellent performance in identifying discriminative
patterns, even with highly sparse datasets. These strategies
circumvent issues like the ‘‘curse of dimensionality’’ and
lack of semantic context by offering compact, expressive
feature vectors. Gualberto et al. [11], for instance, achieved
an F1-score of 99.95% with the XGBoost algorithm using
exclusively the email body, without relying on metadata or
links, demonstrating that well-designed feature engineering
solutions can rival more complex models.
Despite the success of these approaches, many still require
multiple preprocessing steps, feature selection, or topic
extraction, which can increase implementation complexity in
real-world environments. In this context, the present work
proposes a BERT-based model (Bidirectional Encoder Repre-
sentations from Transformers), which eliminates the need for
manual feature engineering and provides robust contextual
representations directly from email text. Moreover, this
study investigates the impact of class balancing techniques
such as undersampling and SMOTE on model performance,
a topic often overlooked in previous Transformer-based
research. By combining a pretrained language model with
data imbalance handling strategies, this approach aims to
offer a practical, scalable, and accurate solution for automatic
phishing detection in emails, making a significant advance-
ment over previous NLP-based or manually engineered
supervised models.
In this work, we propose the PHILDER (PHishing Intel-
ligent Lightweight DEtection on Resource-limited devices)
model, a BERT-variant-based approach aimed at auto-
matic detection of phishing emails in resource-constrained
scenarios. Using exclusively message textual content and
exploring data balancing techniques such as undersampling
and SMOTE, the research evaluates five BERT-based model
variations in terms of predictive performance and compu-
tational efficiency. Through the composite metrics TES,
IES, and RTDE, we demonstrate that the PHILDER model,
derived from TinyBERT with undersampling, offers the best
balance between accuracy and computational cost, standing
out as a practical, scalable, and highly effective solution for
real-world cybersecurity applications.

II. RELATED WORK
The identification of related work followed the approach
described by Bispo [12]. A search was conducted in the
Scopus database using the keywords phishing detection,
restricted to the Computer Science area, and considering
only publications from the last five years. This resulted in
839 articles, of which the ten most relevant were selected
for analysis.
This section organizes the related work based on the char-
acteristics of the phishing detection techniques employed,
primarily categorizing them into Machine Learning and Deep
Learning approaches. This classification aims to provide a
clearer understanding of the methodologies and the current
landscape of research in the field.
A. MACHINE LEARNING APPROACHES
Various studies have explored the use of machine learn-
ing algorithms for effective phishing detection, leveraging
these models’ ability to identify complex patterns in
large volumes of data. The application of Natural Lan-
guage Processing (NLP) techniques in conjunction with
these algorithms has proven particularly promising for
analyzing textual and behavioral characteristics of URLs
and emails.
The most cited paper proposes a phishing detection system
based on seven machine learning algorithms: Decision Tree,
Adaboost, K-star, kNN, Random Forest, SMO, and Naive
Bayes [1]. The system analyzes a set of features that
includes NLP techniques, which showed superior perfor-
mance compared to simple word vectors. The combination
of these approaches resulted in a significant increase in
model accuracy. The results reinforce the effectiveness of
applying machine learning algorithms in conjunction with
NLP techniques for phishing detection, highlighting the
importance of synergy between advanced analysis methods
and predictive models to improve accuracy and efficiency in
identifying fraudulent sites.
The work by [13] proposes the Hybrid Ensemble Feature
Selection (HEFS), a novel approach for feature selection
in phishing detection systems based on machine learning.

by precision, recall, and AUC metrics. Sixteen algorithms
were tested, with GradientBoostingClassifier and Random-

SGD underperformed. The study suggests that combining
learning techniques like bagging and boosting can improve
phishing detection accuracy. Future research should include
more variables and hybrid approaches to optimize predictive
capabilities and reduce errors.
The study by [16] used a phishing site dataset from the UCI
Machine Learning Repository, comprising 30 technical and
behavioral site features. Accuracy was the main performance
metric. The proposed model outperformed existing technolo-
gies, demonstrating high accuracy. Feature selection was
applied to reduce the dataset to the most relevant variables,
ensuring irrelevant attributes did not compromise prediction
quality. An ensemble learning technique combined different
learning models, with final decisions made by majority voting
to avoid single-classifier bias.
Between 2006 and 2016, email classification studies
focused on spam and phishing identification and message
categorization. Datasets such as PU, PhishingCorpus, and
Enron were widely used, exploring header, content, and link
features. Supervised learning methods, particularly SVM,
emerged as the most promising approach, with metrics
like accuracy, recall, precision, and F1-score employed
for performance evaluation. Despite progress, challenges
remain, including new attack types and the need for
better datasets. Future research may extend analysis to
emails in other languages and classify different message
types [17].
B. DEEP LEARNING APPROACHES
Deep learning, with its complex neural network architectures,
has revolutionized phishing detection, enabling multi-layered
data analysis and the extraction of high-level features that
would be difficult to identify with traditional methods.
These approaches are particularly effective in detecting
sophisticated and constantly evolving threats.
In the next paper, the authors propose a cloud-based
solution to strengthen Internet of Things (IoT) device security
by detecting both phishing and Botnet attacks [18]. The
method employs two complementary security models: a
Deep Convolutional Neural Network (DCNN), implemented
directly on IoT devices to identify phishing and DDoS
attacks, and an LSTM model in the backend, responsible for
detecting Botnets and using data extracted by the DCNN to
recognize distributed phishing attacks. Models were trained
with datasets containing phishing and non-phishing URLs,
as well as botnet attack specifics. Results show that the
DCNN efficiently detected phishing on IoT devices, while
the LSTM performed well in backend Botnet identification.
Model integration optimized resource usage, minimized
communication needs, and enabled more effective distributed
attack detection, reinforcing the robustness of the proposed
approach.
The study [19] proposes a model combining Graph Convo-
lutional Networks (GCN) with NLP to detect phishing emails
by analyzing email body content, an area often overlooked
by traditional detection methods. The model achieved high
true positive rates and low false positives, excelling in metrics
such as true positive rate, true negative rate, recall, and preci-
sion, outperforming other deep learning approaches. It high-
lights the importance of analyzing email body text to improve
accuracy and shows promise for detecting new and emerging
phishing threats.
Tang and Mahmoud [20] present a real-time phishing site
detection solution implemented as a user-friendly browser
extension. The proposal stands out for its independence
from external services and high-quality data collected via
user feedback, enabling continuous model improvement. Key
features include instant alerts for suspicious pages, a com-
bination of site blocklist checks and machine learning to
reduce false alarms, and logging for ongoing enhancements.

III. PHILDER: PHISHING INTELLIGENT LIGHTWEIGHT
DETECTION ON RESOURCES-LIMITED DEVICES
This section describes the methodology for automatic
phishing detection in emails. Figure 1 presents the meth-
ods and procedures used to carry out phishing detection
research in emails through natural language processing
models. The advancement of natural language models has
enabled the application of sophisticated threat detection
techniques in electronic communications such as email
phishing. However, using large models can be impractical
in hardware-constrained or real-time environments. In this
context, lightweight BERT-derived models have emerged as
promising alternatives.
A. DATABASE CONSOLIDATION
For the development of an email phishing detection model
based on supervised artificial intelligence, it is essential
to have a pre-labeled dataset composed of representative
examples of both legitimate and fraudulent emails. In this
work, two publicly available datasets widely recognized in
the scientific literature were employed:
• PhishTank: A collaborative platform maintained by
the OpenDNS community, dedicated to the collec-
tion and verification of URLs and emails used in
phishing attacks. Samples are submitted by users
and subsequently validated through community voting.
In this study, 501 confirmed malicious emails were
utilized, exhibiting typical characteristics of phishing
campaigns—such as attempts to steal banking cre-
dentials, service identity spoofing, and redirection to
fraudulent web pages. The fields available in this dataset
include: sender, subject, email body (text), suspicious
URLs, and the class label (phishing).

B. DATA PREPROCESSING
The preprocessing of emails is a crucial step to ensure that the
data are structured efficiently for the Deep Learning model.
In this phase, we applied a set of techniques to remove noise
and standardize the text.
• SMTP Header Removal: Only the email body was
retained, eliminating sender, recipient, intermediate
server information, and routing metadata.
• URL and Email Address Removal: Email addresses
and links were replaced with generic placeholders to
prevent the model from learning specific patterns that
do not generalize well.
• Text Normalization: Content was converted to low-
ercase and special characters were removed, ensuring
uniformity in the model input.
• Stopword Removal: Words irrelevant to classification,
such as pronouns and conjunctions, were removed to
reduce data dimensionality.
After this stage, a DataFrame was constructed containing
the cleaned texts and their respective classes (0 for ham
and 1 for spam/phishing), ready to be processed by the
balancing strategies.
C. DATABASE BALANCING
The original dataset exhibited a significant imbalance,
containing 2,501 legitimate (ham) emails and 501 emails
classified as spam/phishing. Deep learning models tend to
be influenced by the majority class, impairing the detection
of fraudulent emails. To mitigate this issue, we adopted two
distinct balancing strategies.

In the undersampling technique, we reduced the number
of majority-class (ham) emails to balance them with those
of the minority class (spam/phishing). To this end, we ran-
domly selected 502 legitimate emails while retaining the
501 spam/phishing emails. This approach seeks to prevent
majority-class bias but may lead to the loss of valuable
information due to the reduction of legitimate data.
For the oversampling strategy, we employed the Synthetic
Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), which syn-
thesizes new minority-class samples based on the existing
distribution. However, since SMOTE operates solely on
numerical data, it was necessary first to convert the email
texts into vectors using TF-IDF (Term Frequency-Inverse
Document Frequency). After applying SMOTE, we obtained
a final dataset containing 2,501 legitimate emails and 2,501
spam/phishing emails. The class distribution in the dataset is
shown in Figure 2.
After balancing, email texts were converted into numerical
representations compatible with the BERT-base-uncased
model. We used the standard BERT tokenizer, which seg-
ments the text into tokens and applies padding to standardize
sequence lengths.
The data were then split into 70% training and 30% testing
sets using the datasets library from Hugging Face to
ensure compatibility with the model. For each balancing
approach, we created independent training and testing sets.
A large proportion of lightweight models employed in
Natural Language Processing (NLP) tasks—such as phishing
email detection—are derived from the BERT architecture
(Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers).
BERT is a pre-trained NLP model designed to understand
the context surrounding each token. Released by Google
AI in 2018, it is widely accessible for various applications.
Unlike earlier NLP models that only considered left-context,
BERT’s bidirectional and unsupervised pre-training allows
it to learn from both left and right contexts using simple
text corpora [22]. Its application of the Masked Language
Model (MLM) represented a significant advance in NLP,
offering a straightforward implementation compared to other
models and proving to be a powerful tool for language
understanding [23].
Given the limitations of unidirectional models, which
only consider preceding context, BERT’s bidirectional design
offers superior interpretation of text, context, and even
sentiment—hence its designation as a masked model capable
of predicting bidirectional context. BERT operates in two
main phases: pre-training and fine-tuning [22].
During pre-training, BERT performs two tasks: Masked
Language Modeling (MLM) and Next Sentence Prediction
(NSP). In the MLM task, the model predicts the original
tokens that were masked, leveraging the context of the
surrounding words. This deep learning process requires the
model to infer the meaning and context of words to accurately
predict the masked tokens. In the NSP task, the model learns
to predict sentence order by being given pairs of sentences
and determining whether the second sentence follows the
first, thereby enhancing its understanding of inter-sentence
relationships. After pre-training, BERT is fine-tuned on
task-specific datasets, which refines and improves its perfor-
mance. Architecturally, BERT is built upon the Transformer
framework, comprising multiple encoding layers [22].
As the foundation for many derivative models, BERT
demonstrates strong performance in text inference and
classification by effectively capturing sentence relationships
and contextual nuances. In cybersecurity contexts—such as
analyzing and classifying email content, URLs, and web
pages to detect patterns of fraudulent attacks—BERT and its
variants have become essential tools.
To identify the best-performing variant, we tested five
BERT-based models that retain the core Transformer and
bidirectional attention mechanisms but incorporate structural
modifications or compression techniques to reduce compu-
tational complexity. DistilBERT uses knowledge distillation
to achieve approximately 97% of BERT-base’s performance
with only 60% of its parameters [24]. TinyBERT follows
a similar two-stage distillation approach with even fewer
layers, making it ideal for hardware-constrained envi-
ronments [25]. MobileBERT introduces bottleneck layers
and linear projections to optimize execution on mobile
devices [26]. ALBERT employs parameter sharing and
embedding factorization to drastically reduce parameter
count without significant performance loss [27]. Finally,
MiniLM adopts a more compact self-attention mechanism,
delivering high accuracy with notable computational effi-
ciency [28]. These models strike an excellent balance
between performance and computational cost, making them
particularly suitable for real-time and scalable security
applications in resource-limited settings.
Each model exhibits distinct architectural features, making
some variants more advantageous depending on the deploy-
ment scenario. Table 2 provides a comparative overview of
the main differences among the tested BERT models.

D. PERFORMANCE ASSESSMENT
The evaluation of the trained models was conducted in two
stages. The first stage was based on four primary metrics:
accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. The purpose of
this evaluation is to determine which model delivers the
best performance in phishing-email detection. The second
stage addresses each model’s computational efficiency, since
some models may achieve excellent phishing detection
performance at the expense of high computational cost; in
practical deployments, it is therefore preferable to select
models that offer comparable detection performance while
remaining computationally efficient.
The metrics are defined as follows: Accuracy, which
measures the overall proportion of correct classifications
made by the model; Precision, which represents the rate
of true positives among all phishing predictions made by
the model; Recall, which measures the model’s ability
to correctly identify all phishing emails; and F1-score,
which computes the harmonic mean of precision and recall,
providing a more robust measure in scenarios with class
imbalance.
The computational efficiency metrics were assessed in
three stages. The first is the Training Efficiency Score (TES):
this metric denotes the ratio of model accuracy to training
time (Accuracy / Training Time). TES indicates how
effectively a model can learn under a constrained training
budget—a requirement essential for systems that must
be retrained frequently or deployed on resource-limited
devices.
The second is the Inference Efficiency Score (IES): defined
as the ratio of accuracy to total testing time (Accuracy /
Testing Time), IES measures the model’s efficiency when
applied to unseen data. This metric is especially relevant for
evaluating batch-processing detection pipelines, common in
real-world email-filtering systems.
Finally, the third metric is Real-Time Detection Effi-
ciency
(RTDE):
calculated

ity to deliver accurate predictions under strict latency
constraints—a critical factor in real-time phishing detection
within secure email gateways or client-side anti-phishing
modules.

To further contextualize PHILDER’s performance, we con-
ducted a comparative analysis with recent studies on phishing
detection. Although direct comparisons are challenging due
to variations in datasets, evaluation metrics, and experimental
setups, our goal is to demonstrate PHILDER’s competitive
advantage, especially regarding its lightweight nature and
efficiency on resource-constrained devices.
It is observed in Table 1 that many studies focus on
URL-based phishing detection. For example, Sahingoz et al.
[1] achieved 97.98% accuracy with NLP-based features for
URLs. Tang and Mahmoud [20] reported 99.18% accuracy
using Deep Learning (RNN-GRU) for phishing detection
in URLs. Although these results are high, they focus on
URLs, while PHILDER excels in email phishing detection.
For email phishing detection, Alhogail and Alsabih [19]
obtained 98.20% accuracy using NLP. PHILDER, with
99.27% accuracy, surpasses this result, demonstrating supe-
rior performance in the same problem category. Furthermore,
while many works focus solely on accuracy, PHILDER also
emphasizes computational efficiency, a crucial aspect for
resource-constrained devices, which is not widely addressed
in many of the compared studies. The combination of high
accuracy and low per-sample latency (12.9811 ms) positions
PHILDER as a robust and practical solution for production
environments with resource limitations.
It can be observed in Table 3 that oversampling generally
produced superior accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score
compared to undersampling and the original data; however,
oversampling also incurred the highest computational cost
for training, testing, and per-sample detection. Consequently,
we proposed evaluating the models using the TES, IES, and
RTDE metrics. These composite measures allow comparison
of models by both predictive performance and computational
efficiency, guiding the selection of the most appropriate
architecture for real-time phishing detection on resource-
constrained devices.
The oversampling approach was found to present three
main issues: increased computational cost, risk of overfitting,
and comparatively inferior robustness. By artificially expand-
ing the training set—either by replicating minority-class
instances or generating synthetic examples—oversampling
significantly raises both training and inference times, under-
mining TES, IES, and RTDE, which are critical in constrained
environments. Moreover, oversampling may encourage mem-
orization over generalization, thereby masking true accuracy
and weakening real-world phishing detection robustness.
While we acknowledge that parameter tuning for SMOTE
could potentially mitigate some of these issues, our
focus was on finding a solution that inherently balanced
performance with the strict computational constraints of

V. CONCLUSION
Phishing detection is an essential task in the current
cybersecurity landscape, given the growing connectivity and
the transmission of sensitive information across numerous
networked devices. Developing improvements that enhance
device security is critical, as cyberattacks become increas-
ingly sophisticated and damaging. While attacks are growing
more destructive and harder to detect, it is indispensable

to advance technologies that prevent, detect, and mitigate
cybercrime. Thus, the different approaches tested in this study
make a significant contribution to the literature by exploring
deep learning-based alternatives and improvements for email
phishing detection.
The primary contribution of this article is that efficient
phishing detection requires more than comparing traditional
metrics; it is essential to consider a model’s suitability for
real-world contexts, where high computational resources may
not be available and real-time responses are often required.
Therefore, a model must not only be accurate but also
efficient to be truly applicable.
The results for the PHILDER model-based on the three
composite metrics (TES, IES, and RTDE) — show that it is
the most advantageous solution overall. PHILDER achieves
the highest training efficiency (TES), the best batch infer-
ence performance (IES), and excellent real-time efficiency
(RTDE), offering the optimal balance of accuracy, speed,
and resource economy. This makes it highly recommended
for phishing detection applications in hardware-constrained,
real-time environments. MiniLM-family models also proved
competitive, particularly in scenarios demanding extreme
lightweight deployment and rapid inference.
As discussed, the ALBERT, DistilBERT, and Mobile-
BERT models exhibited traditional metric values (accuracy,
precision, recall, and F1-score) close to those of the best
models (PHILDER and MiniLM), which could misleadingly
suggest their suitability for deployment. However, achieving
comparable performance with these models entails a high
computational cost, rendering their use impractical in con-
strained environments.
For future research, it would be valuable to incorporate
additional deep learning models for phishing detection and
evaluate them using TES, IES, and RTDE, comparing their
performance with the results of this study. Such efforts would
significantly advance the cybersecurity field by identifying
increasingly advanced and efficient solutions.
